The C4 CO₂ pump

A short guide to the two models, what they are meant to show you, and where to read further.

The problem

Rubisco is not specific. It carboxylates RuBP with CO₂ and it oxygenates RuBP with O₂, and the two gases compete for the same active site. At 25 °C in today's air, a C3 leaf runs roughly one oxygenation for every three or four carboxylations. Recovering the 2-phosphoglycolate that oxygenation produces costs carbon, ATP and reducing power, and the ratio gets worse as temperature rises, because CO₂ becomes less soluble than O₂ and Rubisco's specificity falls at the same time.

No plant has solved this by improving Rubisco. What C4 plants do instead is change the gas mixture the enzyme sees.

The solution

Hatch and Slack found in 1966 that in sugarcane the first stable product of fixation is not 3-phosphoglycerate but a four-carbon acid, labelled in C-4. The pathway that explains this is a two-cell pump:

  • In the mesophyll, carbonic anhydrase converts CO₂ to bicarbonate, and PEP carboxylase fixes the bicarbonate onto phosphoenolpyruvate to give a four-carbon acid. PEP carboxylase has no oxygenase activity and no affinity problem at these concentrations.
  • The acid diffuses through plasmodesmata into the bundle sheath, where a decarboxylase releases the CO₂ again.
  • Rubisco sits only in the bundle sheath, and now works in a compartment holding far more CO₂ than the air outside.
  • The three-carbon skeleton returns to the mesophyll and pyruvate,Pi dikinase regenerates PEP at a cost of two ATP.

Those two ATP are the price. A C3 leaf spends three ATP per carboxylation; a C4 leaf spends five, and gets in return a Rubisco that has almost stopped oxygenating.

Why the leak is the whole story

The bundle sheath is not sealed. CO₂ concentrated inside it diffuses back out, and the fraction of PEP carboxylase flux that escapes this way — φ = L/Vp, leakiness — sits somewhere near a fifth in a healthy leaf. The pump therefore has to run faster than net assimilation, which is called overcycling, and every wasted turn still costs its two ATP.

This is the thing the first model is for. Open it, take the wall conductance from 5 to 20, and watch bundle sheath CO₂ collapse while the ATP bill climbs. The pathway's advantage is not the pump; it is the pump together with a barrier good enough to make the pumping worth paying for.

Jenkins, Furbank and Hatch built the model this simulation follows, and predicted around 13% leakage with the C4 cycle running some 15% faster than net photosynthesis. Hatch, Agostino and Jenkins later measured the leak directly, chasing a 14CO₂ pulse with unlabelled CO₂ and watching label come back out of the leaf.

Why the anatomy has to be built the way it is

Kranz anatomy looks like a decorative detail in a textbook cross-section. It is not. The second model lets you rearrange it, and five separate requirements show up as five different ways of failing:

  • Capture and release must be in different cells. Put PEP carboxylase and the decarboxylase together and the cycle still turns — dozens of times a second — but transports nothing at all while spending the ATP anyway.
  • Rubisco must be in the cell the pump fills. Otherwise the bundle sheath is pressurised with CO₂ that nothing consumes, and all of it leaks back.
  • Only the mesophyll touches the air. Put everything in the bundle sheath and the leaf starves, because the barrier that holds CO₂ in also keeps it out.
  • The wall needs a diffusion barrier. In NADP-ME and PCK grasses this is a suberised lamella, perfectly correlated with photosynthetic type in the 42 grasses Hattersley and Browning surveyed.
  • The cells need unusually many plasmodesmata. The shuttle carries more flux than net photosynthesis, in both directions. C4 monocots have several times the plasmodesmatal density of their C3 relatives at this interface.

Where the models stop

Each tool lists its own simplifications, and those lists are worth reading as carefully as the results. Four are worth flagging here because they touch the conclusions you might otherwise draw:

  • Temperature is fixed at 25 °C. The C3/C4 crossover with temperature, which is most of the ecological story, is outside both models.
  • One decarboxylation type only. Real C4 plants come in NADP-ME, NAD-ME and PCK flavours that move reductant between the cells differently.
  • The suberised lamella is not universal. NAD-ME grasses do not have one, yet their leakiness is comparable — so a long symplastic path can apparently do the same job as a suberin barrier.
  • Carbonic anhydrase is treated as all-or-nothing. In the sandbox, moving it out of the mesophyll wrecks the leaf. In maize, a double mutant with under 3% of wild-type activity photosynthesises normally at today's CO₂ and only suffers below it. The sandbox exaggerates a real requirement; the reading below shows how much.

Reading

Start here

  • von Caemmerer, S. & Furbank, R. T. (2003) The C4 pathway: an efficient CO₂ pump. Photosynthesis Research 77: 191–207. The clearest short account of the pump as a pump. Read this first.
  • Hatch, M. D. (1987) C4 photosynthesis: a unique blend of modified biochemistry, anatomy and ultrastructure. Biochimica et Biophysica Acta 895: 81–106. The synthesis by the person who found the pathway. Still the reference point.
  • Kanai, R. & Edwards, G. E. (1999) The biochemistry of C4 photosynthesis. In: Sage, R. F. & Monson, R. K. (eds) C4 Plant Biology, Academic Press, pp. 49–87. Where the three decarboxylation subtypes are laid out properly.

The original observation

  • Hatch, M. D. & Slack, C. R. (1966) Photosynthesis by sugar-cane leaves. A new carboxylation reaction and the pathway of sugar formation. Biochemical Journal 101: 103–111. Four-second 14CO₂ exposures, label in C-4 of malate and aspartate. Short, and worth reading in the original.

The pool and the leak

  • Furbank, R. T. & Hatch, M. D. (1987) Mechanism of C4 photosynthesis: the size and composition of the inorganic carbon pool in bundle sheath cells. Plant Physiology 85: 958–964.
  • Furbank, R. T., Jenkins, C. L. D. & Hatch, M. D. (1989) CO₂ concentrating mechanism of C4 photosynthesis: permeability of isolated bundle sheath cells to inorganic carbon. Plant Physiology 91: 1364–1371. Where the conductance in the first model's leakiness dial comes from.
  • Jenkins, C. L. D., Furbank, R. T. & Hatch, M. D. (1989) Mechanism of C4 photosynthesis: a model describing the inorganic carbon pool in bundle sheath cells. Plant Physiology 91: 1372–1381. The leakiness-and-overcycling argument, worked through.
  • Hatch, M. D., Agostino, A. & Jenkins, C. L. D. (1995) Measurement of the leakage of CO₂ from bundle-sheath cells of leaves during C4 photosynthesis. Plant Physiology 108: 173–181.
  • Farquhar, G. D. (1983) On the nature of carbon isotope discrimination in C4 species. Australian Journal of Plant Physiology 10: 205–226. Why δ¹³C can be read as a measurement of leakiness. The route to testing all this on a real plant.

The anatomy

The models themselves

  • von Caemmerer, S. & Furbank, R. T. (1999) Modeling C4 photosynthesis. In: Sage, R. F. & Monson, R. K. (eds) C4 Plant Biology, Academic Press, San Diego, pp. 173–211. The flux equations both tools are built on.

Evolution and context

  • Sage, R. F. (2004) The evolution of C4 photosynthesis. New Phytologist 161: 341–370.
  • Sage, R. F., Christin, P.-A. & Edwards, E. J. (2011) The C4 plant lineages of planet Earth. Journal of Experimental Botany 62: 3155–3169. More than sixty independent origins. Whatever the models make look difficult to assemble, evolution has assembled repeatedly.
